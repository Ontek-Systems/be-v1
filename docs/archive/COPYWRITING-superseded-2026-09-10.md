# Blissful Escapes Copywriting Guide

The single source of truth for every word on the site. Written from Emma's second review call
(`emma 2nd call.txt`, 1 Sept 2026) and from an audit of the old Blissful Escapes site.

**How to use this file:** read it, then write the copy and apply it. Do not produce a written
audit of what is wrong with the existing copy, do not list violations back, and do not ask for
sign off on wording before editing. Read the file, apply §3 (headlines), §4 (rhythm) and §5 (the page brief), run the
output through the Kill List (§10) and the Checklist (§12) in your head, and make the edits.

Report afterwards, briefly: what changed, anything genuinely blocked (a fact nobody has yet),
and any judgement call worth a second opinion. Copy is cheap to revise once it is on the page
and expensive to review in the abstract.

**How to hold these rules.** This file is a brief, not a specification. Applied literally and
uniformly it produces exactly the failure it is meant to prevent: copy that is technically
compliant and obviously machine written. The Kill List (§10) and the factual rules are hard, so
treat those as absolute. Everything else, the sentence patterns, the worked examples, the voice
notes, is guidance for calibration, and where following a rule to the letter would make a
sentence worse, the sentence wins. If you notice yourself assembling copy by satisfying rules
one at a time, stop, because that is the sound of it going wrong. Write the paragraph as a
person would say it, then check it against the file.

## 1. Who is speaking

Blissful Escapes is Emma Carrigan, from Ormskirk, Lancashire, four years in luxury travel,
thirty five years in travel and client facing business before that. Sylvia now works alongside
her and has her own clients around Wigan. Two named people. That is the whole company, and
that is the entire selling proposition.

Everything on this site must sound like **one experienced person talking to one person who is
about to spend a lot of money on a trip that matters to them.** Not a brand. Not an agency.
Not a marketing department.

Emma's own words on how she wants to come across:

> "People love relatability. People always say the actual strength, you're very down to earth.
> People can talk to you about anything. You can have a laugh with people. You're not stuffy."

> "We'll come away from the corporate bullshit."

And on the level of craft she is paying for:

> "Some of that copy on my old website, I paid a lot of money for. There's some descriptions in
> there that sound like they've just come out of a travel magazine. Use it."

So the standard is high. The destination writing should stand next to a travel magazine, and
everything about Emma, the service and the money should be plainly and specifically true. Both
in the same voice, which is set out in §2.

**Emma's warmth is a fact about Emma, not a licence for chatty copy.** This is the single most
important correction in this file, because getting it wrong has now failed twice. Emma says she
is down to earth and not stuffy. That describes how she is on the phone with a client. It does
not mean the website should sound casual, folksy, or conversational. It should sound like a
professional firm that happens to be run by warm people. The warmth arrives through what we say,
Emma and Sylvia by name, Ormskirk, seven days a week, and never through chatty phrasing.

## 2. The voice

There is one voice. An earlier version of this file split it into two registers and that split
caused every serious failure the copy has had, because the wrong register kept landing in the
wrong place. It is gone. One voice, everywhere, from the hero to the form labels to the
destination pages.

**The voice is: professional, descriptive, specific, and flowing.**

The benchmark is the line Emma singled out unprompted as the best copy on the site:

> "Boutique travel planning, personally handled. Based in Ormskirk, Lancashire, available seven
> days a week."

Her verdict: *"I love that. That's the type of thing that's really personal, slightly different."*

It names what the business is, who does it, where they are and when you can reach them. Four
facts, no adjectives straining to do the work, and it still reads warm, because the facts
themselves are warm. Everything on this site should be recognisably the same species as that
line.

### What that means in practice

- **Say the specific thing.** "Ormskirk, Lancashire" beats "the North West". "Seven days a week"
  beats "always here for you". "Over 600 suppliers" beats "extensive partnerships".
- **Professional, not chatty.** No slang, no idiom, no wordplay. Emma being warm on the phone
  is not a licence to write casually.
- **Flowing, not clipped.** Varied sentence length, thoughts joined with and, but, so, because,
  which. See §4, because this is the failure that recurs most.
- **Never claim what cannot be checked.** No "award winning" without an award.
- **Never answer an objection the reader has not raised.** "There is nobody reading off a script
  when you ring" is defensive and strange, because nobody was wondering. State what we do, not
  what we are not. This applies to hold music, call centres, scripts, and every other complaint
  inherited from the old site.
- **First person plural for the business.** "We". Use "I" only inside a bio or a quote that is
  explicitly Emma speaking.
- **Contractions are fine** where they read naturally.

### Subject changes, voice does not

Destination copy will naturally carry more sensory detail than a contact form, because a place
has more to describe than a form does. That is the subject changing, not the voice. The register
stays professional and specific throughout, and the descriptive writing simply has more concrete
nouns available to it.

The model for that descriptive writing is the copy Emma paid for on the old site:

> "From the lush rural heartland away from the vibrant cities to the outlying rice paddies and
> tropical forests, Thailand is a glistening gem. With two coastlines and 1400 jungle topped
> islands, you can party, dance, meditate and rejuvenate on Thailand's coast and islands."

> "The beauty of safaris is the opportunity to fall into the natural rhythm of the bush. Enjoy
> twice daily wildlife activities, then take a well deserved siesta beneath the warm African sun."

What to take from it: concrete nouns and real numbers rather than "stunning scenery"; the
experience described in the order a person would live it; second person, present tense, so the
reader is already there; and at most one flourish per paragraph before returning to plain
description.

Length for descriptive copy: destination intros 90 to 140 words, sub sections 50 to 90 words.
Never a wall of text.

## 3. Headlines

Two attempts at this site's headlines have failed in opposite directions. Read both failure
modes before writing anything, because the correct register sits between them and it is narrow.

**Failure mode one: operational flatness.** Headlines that describe how the business works.
"We plan a few regions properly, not everywhere badly." "Your holiday, planned by a person you
can ring." These read like a utilities company.

**Failure mode two: chatty vagueness.** Overcorrecting into warmth and ending up twee.
"Postcards from our travellers." "Honeymoons, safaris, and the big ones in between." "We plan
every trip ourselves." Tom's verdict on these: *"sounds like a 10 year old is writing this."*

### The target register

**Professional, descriptive, and specific. Say plainly what the thing is, in industry language,
without slang and without straining for feeling.**

The benchmark is the line Emma singled out unprompted as the best copy on the site:

> "Boutique travel planning, personally handled. Based in Ormskirk, Lancashire, available seven
> days a week."

Study what that does. It names the category (boutique travel planning). It names the
differentiator (personally handled). It names the place and the availability. It is completely
professional, contains no slang and no wordplay, and it still reads warm because the facts
themselves are warm. **Every headline on this site should be recognisably the same species as
that line.**

Practically:
- Name the category. "Luxury travel planning", "destinations", "specialist trips", not a mood.
- Elevated but plain vocabulary. "Arranged", "planned in detail", "handled personally",
  "impartial". Not "the big ones", not "by heart", not "postcards".
- A headline may be a noun phrase. It does not need a verb, and it usually reads better without
  one.
- Slightly formal is correct. This is a firm handling five figure bookings.

### Brevity, and the explaining problem

The recurring fault is not vocabulary or register, it is **length**. Copy that explains what the
reader can already see reads as strange and machine written, however well the sentence is built.

**Section labels are nouns.** One or two words. "Destinations". "About us". "Types of trips".
"Gallery". Not "Where we plan", not "From recent trips", not "Destination gallery". The label
tells the reader where they are, nothing more, and anything longer is the copy talking to itself.

The one exception is a label doing real work that a noun cannot do, such as proof ("Five star
rated on Google") or an invitation ("Ready when you are"). Both earn their length. A label that
merely describes the section does not.

**Headings are short.** Under about eight words. A heading above a grid of destination
photographs does not need to announce that these are destination photographs.

Rejected for over explaining:

| Rejected | The problem |
|---|---|
| "A selection of the destinations we plan" | Captions the obvious. The reader can see it is a selection of destinations. |
| "Recent trips, photographed by our clients" | Explains the provenance of images nobody asked about. |
| "Luxury travel planning, handled personally from start to finish" | Three qualifications where one would do. |
| "Destination gallery" (as a label) | Two words explaining a gallery of destinations sitting directly beneath it. |

**Before shipping any heading or label, ask: would a reader looking at this section already know
this?** If yes, cut it down to the noun or delete it.

### One list per sentence

A specific detail earns its place once. The habit of writing a good concrete clause and then
appending a second set of examples to it is the most persistent length fault in this copy.

> "From Capri and the Maldives to Machu Picchu and the Great Barrier Reef, with photographs sent
> back by clients from Iceland, Tanzania and Sicily."

The first list already establishes the range. The second one restates it in a different category
and adds nothing but words. Cut at the point the sentence has made its case:

> "From Capri and the Maldives to Machu Picchu and the Great Barrier Reef, with photographs sent
> back by clients."

When a sentence already has a list in it, the qualifying clause after it stays abstract. Two
lists in one sentence is one too many.

### Brevity is not simplicity

Cutting length is not the same as simplifying the language, and confusing the two produces the
other recurring failure: copy that is short and reads as though it were written for a child.

> "Some from the trips we plan, some sent back by the clients who travelled."

Short, accurate, and juvenile. The "some of this, some of that" construction explains a
distinction nobody needs explained, in the plainest possible words, as though the reader cannot
be trusted with anything else.

The target is **short and assured**. Fewer words, but the words of someone who does this for a
living and does not feel the need to justify the page. Industry vocabulary is welcome where it
is the natural term. "Recent travels" is better than "Some trips we planned" and shorter too.

**Deleting is the answer only when the line has nothing to say.** If a sentence exists purely to
restate what the section obviously is, cut it. But do not reach for deletion because the line is
hard to get right, and never delete an existing section's body copy without being asked. The
better move is almost always to give the line something to carry: real place names, a real
number, a real detail. A gallery intro that lists Capri, the Maldives and Machu Picchu is doing
work. One that says "some trips we planned" is not.

### Worked examples

Every one of these was written for this site and rejected. Learn the pattern, not the lines.

| Rejected | Failure mode | Better |
|---|---|---|
| "Your holiday, planned by a person you can ring" | Operational. Sells a phone line. | "Boutique luxury travel, planned personally" |
| "We plan a few regions properly, not everywhere badly" | Operational, and a disclaimer. | "Destinations we plan in depth" |
| "We plan every trip ourselves" | Chatty. Reads juvenile. | "Luxury travel planning, handled personally from start to finish" |
| "Postcards from our travellers" | Chatty. Twee. | "Recent trips, photographed by our clients" |
| "Honeymoons, safaris, and the big ones in between" | Chatty. "The big ones" is slang. | "The trips we specialise in" |
| "The places we know by heart" | Chatty. Sentimental. | "Destinations we plan in depth" |
| "Tell us about your trip" | Sounds like a review form. | "Start planning your trip" |
| "The whole company is two people" | A body line, not a heading. | "Luxury travel planning, handled personally from start to finish" |
| "Algorithms do not take holidays. We do." | Lifted from Jo Shayler's site. | "Why book with us" |

### Rules

- **No slang, no idiom, no wordplay, no puns.** If it sounds like a slogan or a turn of phrase,
  cut it and describe the thing instead.
- **No sentimentality.** "By heart", "promising yourselves", "postcards" are all reaching for
  feeling. The feeling should come from the destination copy and the client reviews, not the
  headings.
- **Do not put a full stop on a short heading.** It lands flat.
- **Aspiration belongs in the destination and holiday type copy**, where it is earned with
  concrete detail. Section headings on the homepage are descriptive, not aspirational.
- **Never open with a limitation.**

## 4. Rhythm, and how to stop it sounding machine written

The most common complaint about this site's copy has not been the vocabulary or the facts. It
has been the rhythm. Tom's words: *"why does it stop and start so much, it should just flow."*

**The tell is uniform sentence length.** Short declaratives, stacked, each one self contained,
each ending in a full stop that lands like a drum beat. It reads as machine written even when
every individual sentence is fine.

Written for this site, and wrong:

> "There is no call centre and no script. When you ring, one of us answers."

> "We plan a defined set of regions rather than the entire world, and we have travelled all of
> them. That is how we can advise on which coast to stay on and which month to avoid."

Both say the right thing in the wrong shape. Two separate thoughts that a person would have run
together into one:

> "Between the two of us we handle everything ourselves, so there is no call centre to get
> through and nobody reading off a script when you ring."

### How to fix it

- **Vary the length deliberately.** A paragraph of three sentences should not have three
  sentences of similar length. Aim for one long, one medium, one short, in any order. If two
  consecutive sentences are within a few words of each other, join one pair or split another.
- **Join thoughts with conjunctions instead of full stops.** "and", "so", "because", "which",
  "though". A full stop between two closely related thoughts creates the staccato. Subordinate
  clauses are what make prose sound human.
- **Let sentences carry more than one idea.** The instinct to give every fact its own sentence is
  what produces the machine cadence.
- **Avoid the parallel triple.** "The right hotel on the right route at the right price."
  "No call centre, no script, no nonsense." Rhetorically neat and instantly recognisable as
  generated. One rule of three per page at most, and preferably none.
- **Avoid the fragment as a punchline.** A short sentence dropped after a longer one for effect
  is a real device, but used more than once or twice on a page it becomes the house style of
  every AI written landing page in existence.
- **Read it aloud.** If you run out of breath, good. If it sounds like a list of statements
  being read out, rewrite it.

Model the cadence on the destination copy Emma paid for (§2), which does this naturally:

> "The beauty of safaris is the opportunity to fall into the natural rhythm of the bush. Enjoy
> twice daily wildlife activities, then take a well deserved siesta beneath the warm African sun."

Long clause, comma, continuation. It breathes.

## 5. What each page has to do

### Hero
One promise, one sub line, one action. The promise is not a destination and not a price, it is
**the person doing the planning**. Sub line should carry a proof fact (five star rated, ABTA and
ATOL protected, seven days a week). The form is an **enquiry**, never a booking. Emma was clear
on this:

> "This is not going to enable people to book holidays."

So no "Book now", no "Search", no "Check availability". The verb is **plan**, **tell us**, or
**start**.

### About / Emma and Sylvia
Two bios, first person, written by Emma (she is supplying them). The site's job is to frame them.
Frame with real life, not credentials: Sicily in August with the crowds, Morocco, the camel, the
recce flight to Cyprus to look at wedding venues with the actual clients (booked for September
2026, so frame it as coming up, not done). Emma on why:

> "It's real, isn't it? It's real."

Photography brief follows the copy brief: casual holiday pictures of Emma and Sylvia, not a
corporate headshot. *"Too perfect, too polished."*

### Why book with us (About page, not the homepage)
This section lives on the About page. It was briefly built onto the homepage and removed: the
homepage already carries the proof through the reviews count and the accreditations, and a five
reason argument there duplicates the About page rather than adding to it.

Five reasons, numbered, each one a short heading in Emma's voice plus two or three sentences.
The five, adapted from the structure Emma admired on Jo Shayler's site (see §7) but rewritten
entirely in her own terms, are:

1. **You get a real person.** Named, reachable, seven days a week, on WhatsApp if that is easier.
2. **You get a specialist.** Honeymoons, safaris, milestone trips, proposals, destination
   weddings. Say the specialisms, do not say "we do everything".
3. **You are protected.** ABTA, ATOL, and the backing of Azure Indies as a tour operator. State
   the numbers.
4. **You get the connections.** Over six hundred suppliers, and the ability to pull a string when
   something goes wrong at eleven at night in another time zone.
5. **You get straight answers on money.** No hidden fees, no admin charges, no premium for the
   service. Say it plainly because everyone else fudges it.

Heading for the section: **"Why book with us"** or **"Why book with Emma"**. Not a pun.

### Destinations
By region, not by country, and no more than ten. Emma:

> "I'd recommend maybe five or ten, because after that it just gets information overload. They
> already know you do everything."

**Important: the region list is not a limit on what Blissful Escapes will book.** They book the
whole world. The list exists to avoid information overload, and it should be framed as the
places clients ask for most often, always with an explicit line inviting anything else. Copy
that presents the list as a restriction ("we plan a defined set of regions rather than the
entire world") is factually wrong and turns business away.

Regions agreed with Emma: Southeast Asia, rest of Asia including the Indian subcontinent, Middle East,
Oceania and Australia, Europe (splittable into Mediterranean and rest of Europe), Caribbean,
North America, South America, Africa. Each gets a descriptive paragraph in the register set out in §2.

### Holiday types
Honeymoons, family, couples, adventure, wellbeing, **solos** (Emma specifically asked for solos
to be added), plus ski, city breaks, safaris, proposals and weddings as the range allows. Each
type gets a descriptive paragraph and a genuinely different angle. Do not write six paragraphs that
are the same paragraph with the noun swapped.

### Testimonials and case studies
Five or six deep case studies, the rest as a carousel. Confirmed case studies: Matt Carrigan
(Zanzibar), Donna Brown, Claire Blackhurst, Helen Jones (Peru and Galapagos, £36k, the biggest
trip Emma has planned), Eddie and Sarah (proposal in Halkidiki, Greece, 19 May), plus the HOA
review. Bethany Flynn is family and Emma would rather lead with independent names.

Case study copy structure, every time:
1. Who and what the occasion was, one line.
2. What they wanted, in their words if possible.
3. What Emma actually did, specifically. The detail is the product.
4. The outcome, ideally in the client's own review text.

Never write a case study that could be about any client. If it does not contain a fact only that
trip had, it is not finished.

Section heading: replace *"The trips they came home talking about"*. Emma: *"It just sounds a bit
basic."* Her own suggestion, which is the right direction, was closer to **"Hear what our clients
have to say"**. Prefer something plainer still: **"What our clients say"**, or lead with the
number, **"Twenty nine five star reviews"**, since the number is the proof.

### Contact
Email, mobile, and **WhatsApp**, all three, at the top of the page and in the header. Two named
contacts with their own numbers: Emma and Sylvia. Emma:

> "Some people will want to deal with Sylvia because she's got her own clients."

Also: seven days a week, Ormskirk. Remove the stray mobile number sitting next to the privacy
policy in the footer, it *"looks a bit random"*. Keep the company number.

### Forms
Email and name mandatory. **Phone optional.** Departure airport matters and stays. Dates need a
"flexible" option, and ideally plus or minus one, two or three days. **No budget field** on the
form, Emma will raise budget in conversation. The free text field should prompt for the occasion:
"Tell us about the trip. Who is travelling, and is it for a special occasion, a wedding, a
honeymoon, a fiftieth?"

## 6. What to take from the old site, and what to bin

Emma paid for roughly half the old copy and wrote the other half herself. She asked for the paid
half to be reused. Here is the split, so nothing good is lost and nothing bad is carried over.

### How to tell the two halves apart

Screenshots of the old site were reviewed on 1 Sept 2026 and the split is legible from the
prose itself. Apply this test to any old page before reusing a word of it.

**Emma's own writing, which goes in the bin.** The markers cluster, and any two of them
together are conclusive:

- Exclamation marks, usually at the end of a paragraph. "Spain really does have it all!"
- Comma splices where a full stop belongs. "Santorini is the number one romantic getaway in
  Greece, See the world famous sunset."
- Typos and stray spaces. "Valetta", "vist", "restuarants", "Agean", "other European counties",
  "Experience Istanbul , vist the Blue Mosque", "The beaches are mostly Pebbly".
- Rhetorical questions as openers. "Wouldn't it be great to go on holiday, and have all the
  stress taken away."
- Factual errors, which the paid copy does not make. Barcelona described as the capital of
  Spain, Granada spelled Grenada, Chania placed on the northeast coast of Crete when it is on
  the northwest, Kefalonia listed twice in the same sentence, the Balos Lagoon conflated with
  the Venetian harbour at Chania.
- Pandemic framing. "We can help you navigate the changes to the current travel policies."

**The paid copy, which is worth lifting.** Sri Lanka is the clearest surviving example:

> "Spectacular train journeys take visitors to remote hill stations, where life runs at a slow
> pace in the rolling hills. Leopards, sloth bears, elephants and sambar provide interesting
> game drives in Yala and Udawalawe. Golden beaches line the southern shores. Relax in a pool
> bale, or take an ocean cruise in search of the great blue whales."

The markers are the mirror image: named specifics a general writer would not reach for (Yala,
Udawalawe, sambar, a pool bale), no exclamation marks anywhere, correct spelling and grammar
throughout, and the experience sequenced in the order a person would live it. It also carries
occasional American spellings, "snorkeling" rather than "snorkelling", which is a useful
fingerprint since Emma writes British English.

**What this means practically.** Of the European country pages, Malta, Greece, Croatia, Turkey,
Spain and Italy are all Emma's own and none of them survive. The paid work sits on the long haul
pages, Thailand, Sri Lanka and the safari pages. Do not assume a page is paid for because it is
long. Check it against this test first.

### Keep and adapt (the paid half)

- **Destination descriptions.** Thailand, and the rest of the country pages in the same voice.
  Concrete detail, sequenced experience. This is the model for all descriptive copy on the site.
- **The safari pages.** "Migration Safaris in East Africa" and "Big 5 Safaris in Southern Africa"
  are the strongest writing on the old site. Genuinely specific: Maasai heritage, the 1.5 million
  strong herds, the Mara River crossings in August and September, Sabi Sands, the Okavango flood
  season, walking safaris in Mana Pools. Lift the substance wholesale, retune the sentences.
- **"Luxury is a feeling."** The concierge page opener. It is the one abstract line on the old
  site that earns itself, because the paragraph under it immediately gets concrete: airport
  transfers, restaurant bookings, your favourite evening drink waiting at the hotel.
- **The concierge specifics.** Transfers, personalised touches, restaurant reservations,
  the evening tipple at the first choice hotel. Small, real, and exactly the sort of thing that
  justifies the fee.
- **"We arrange travel to world class destinations without relying on any particular hotel or
  travel chain."** The impartiality point, and it is a genuine differentiator. Rewrite tighter,
  keep the substance.
- **The bucket list framing.** Taj Mahal, Great Wall, Galapagos, Northern Lights, Orient Express,
  Wimbledon, a Formula 1 weekend. A concrete list of aspirations beats any adjective.

### Bin (the half Emma wrote herself, or that has aged out)

- **The old "Why Us?" page.** Run on sentences, missing spaces ("your money is completely
  safe.You"), stray exclamation marks, and the pandemic framing ("we have yearned to travel during
  the Pandemic and many of us have re-done our bucket lists!"). The pandemic references are dead
  copy in 2026. The *substance* is good and survives into "Why book with us": thirty five years of
  experience, client relationships first, impartial advice, six hundred suppliers, ABTA and ATOL,
  seven days a week, no hold music. Rewrite every sentence.
- **"We don't keep you on hold for ages listening to crazy music!"** Right instinct, wrong
  execution. The point is worth keeping, the phrasing is not.
- **The grey "WHY CHOOSE US?" tick list sidebar.** Generic, and it appeared on every single page.
  "Tailored Impartial Advice", "We support you every step of the way", "100% privacy guaranteed"
  are filler that any agency could claim. If a claim survives, it has to be earned in a sentence,
  not asserted in a tick box.
- **"Part Of Hays Independence Group" and any Holidaysplease reference.** Gone. Replaced by
  Azure Indies. Already removed from the codebase, do not reintroduce.
- **"Travel Bespoke For You".** Word salad.
- **The repeated "ENQUIRE ABOUT [PAGE NAME]" sidebar boxes**, which produced "Enquire about
  Contact Us". Never auto generate a heading from a page title.
- **Every instance of "Get in touch!" with an exclamation mark**, and "Enquire today !" with the
  floating space. One call to action per page, written deliberately.

## 7. The reference site: Jo Shayler Holidays

Emma pulled up a competitor's site on the call and walked through it. It is worth recording
exactly what she liked, because it is the only external example we have of her reacting to
someone else's copy in real time.

**Jo Shayler Holidays** (spelled S H A Y L E R). An Azure Indies colleague of Emma's, trading
for years, an advisory director on the Indies board, and an award winner (Home Based Travel
Agent of the Year).

Emma's summary, and it sets the terms for how we use this:

> "I don't think it's any better than ours, but there's a few little pointers for me in terms of
> the personalisation."

So: take the structure and the nerve, not the words. We are not rewriting Jo's site with Emma's
name on it.

### What she liked, and what we take

**The "Why Book With Me?" page.** This is the page Emma stopped on. Its structure is now the
model for our "Why book with us" section (§5).

It opens by attacking the alternative rather than praising itself:

> "Book a holiday in cyberspace and it comes with a database. Algorithms don't take holidays.
> Travel professionals do, and we take them seriously."

Then five numbered reasons, each phrased as a **first person claim about the person**, not about
the company:

1. I'm a real person
2. I am a specialist
3. I am accredited
4. I am well connected
5. I am transparent

**Why that works, and what to reproduce:**

- The reasons are about a human being, not a service. "I am well connected" lands where "we have
  strong supplier relationships" does not.
- Numbered and short. The reader can scan five headings and get the whole argument.
- The transparency point talks about money directly, which almost no travel site does. Jo's
  version: no hidden fees or admin charges, no premium despite the standard of service,
  competitive prices, and a direct dig at do it yourself booking sites where you think you have
  a price and then get hit with supplements. Emma nodded along to this one specifically. Our
  version of it is reason five, and it must stay this blunt.
- It closes personally: **"Get in touch. I'd love to hear from you"**, then phone, email, social
  links, newsletter signup. An invitation, not a hard sell.

**The casual photography.** Jo has a picture of herself cooking, not a corporate headshot.
This is what triggered Emma's decision on her own photos:

> "That goes back to what we said before about the authenticity. I think we'll come away from the
> corporate bullshit. We'll go for a picture of me on holiday. When I went to Morocco last year,
> I've got pictures of me in Riyadh, pictures of me on the back of a camel."

**The news hub.** Jo runs blogs, newsletters, recent posts and podcasts. Emma noted it as
something we would fill with testimonials and case studies rather than blogging for its own sake.
Not a launch requirement.

### What she thought Jo got wrong

**The awards are buried** in the bottom corner of the page. Emma:

> "I'd be shouting about those a bit more. They're right down in the bottom corner, aren't they?
> I'd have them on the homepage."

Direct instruction for us: proof (the 29 five star reviews, the accreditations, any award) goes
**high on the homepage**, not in the footer. The footer is where the old Blissful Escapes site
hid its credentials and it is part of why it read like a template.

## 8. House style

- **No hyphens in body copy.** Project rule, and it holds here. "World class", "well deserved",
  "jungle topped", "five star". Rephrase rather than hyphenate. Only where omitting one is
  genuinely ungrammatical.
- **No em dashes and no en dashes.** Use a comma, a full stop, or restructure the sentence. If a
  sentence needs a dash to work, it needs rewriting.
- **No exclamation marks.** None. Emma's warmth comes from what she says, not from punctuation.
- **British English.** Personalised, favourite, travelling, speciality, realise, whilst avoided
  in favour of while.
- **Numbers.** Spell out one to ten in body copy, numerals above. Always numerals for money
  (£36,000), distances, dates, and phone numbers. Always numerals when the number is the proof:
  "29 five star reviews", "over 600 suppliers", "35 years".
- **Sentence case for headings.** Not Title Case, and never ALL CAPS body copy. The old site's
  shouty caps headings are part of why it read cheap.
- **Currency** as £36,000, not "36 grand" (that is Emma's speech, not the site's voice).
- **Ormskirk, Lancashire** in full on first mention on any page. It is a trust signal.
- **ABTA and ATOL** in caps, with membership numbers where they appear. IATA, Advantage, Azure
  Indies alongside them.
- Second person for the reader throughout. The reader is "you", never "the client" or "our
  customers" in body copy.

## 9. Sentence patterns that work

Steal these shapes, not these exact words.

**The plain fact opener.** Start a section with something flatly true and slightly unusual, then
explain it.
> "Two people plan every trip here. That is deliberate."

**The named specific.** Replace the category with the instance.
> Not: "We arrange unforgettable wildlife experiences."
> Instead: "Twice daily game drives in the Sabi Sands, then a siesta through the worst of the heat."

**The admitted limit, used carefully.** Credibility can come from what you rule out, but only
where the limit is real. Do not invent one for rhetorical effect, and never imply Blissful
Escapes will not book something. Safe version, about depth of knowledge rather than coverage:
> "These are the regions we are asked for most often, and we have travelled all of them."

**The sequence.** Walk the reader through the day in order.
> "Land in Zanzibar, and the drive to the coast takes an hour. By late afternoon you are in the
> water."

**The client's own line.** A real review sentence, used verbatim and attributed, beats anything
written for them.

## 10. Kill list

Never appears on this site. Includes the specific lines Emma and Tom flagged on the call.

**Killed by name:**
- "Beaches, balloons and bucket lists". The alliterative triple. Dead.
- "Eight regions, known inside out". Flat and operational. Dead. (The problem was the register, not the counting. A number in a headline is fine when it is doing work, as in "29 five star reviews".)
- "The trips they came home talking about". Emma: *"sounds a bit basic"*. Dead.
- "The one trip that has to be right, handled with care". Filler. Dead.
- "Travel Bespoke For You"
- "Part of the Hays Travel Independence Group", "Powered by Holidaysplease"

**Killed by category:**
- Alliterative rule of three lists of any kind. No exceptions, no clever ones.
- "Unforgettable", "breathtaking", "stunning", "hidden gem", "bucket list" as an adjective,
  "curated", "seamless", "elevate", "journey" meaning holiday, "wanderlust", "paradise found",
  "your dream holiday awaits", "let us take care of the rest", "sit back and relax".
- "Nestled." Anywhere. For anything.
- "Whether you're a X or a Y, we've got you covered."
- "In today's world" and every variant.
- Hollow rhetorical questions as headings. "Ready for a holiday?" "What are you waiting for?" A genuine question that the page then answers is fine.
- Any sentence whose content is that the company is passionate.
- Superlatives about the business itself. Emma is confident, not boastful, and she reads
  boastful copy as fake.
- Fake scarcity, countdowns, "limited availability", pulsing live indicators (also a hard
  project rule).
- Booking language on a business that does not take bookings online.
- Emoji.

## 12. Checklist before any copy ships

Run every block through this as you write, not as a report afterwards. If it fails one line,
rewrite it and move on. This is a filter, not a deliverable.

1. Could this sentence appear on a competitor's site with the company name swapped? If yes,
   rewrite until it could not.
2. Is there at least one concrete, checkable fact in every paragraph?
3. Does this sound like the benchmark line in §2, or has it drifted casual or operational?
4. Read it aloud. Does it sound like Emma talking, or like a brand?
3a. Is this heading or label explaining something the reader can already see on the page?
   Cut it to a noun (§3).
4a. Are the sentences in this paragraph all roughly the same length? If so, it will read as
   machine written. Join a pair with a conjunction or vary them (§4).
5. Any hyphens, em dashes, en dashes, or exclamation marks? Remove.
6. Any word from the Kill List? Remove.
7. Any alliteration doing the work that a fact should be doing? Remove.
8. Any claim that cannot be evidenced by a review, a membership number, or a real trip? Cut it
   or evidence it.
8a. **Does the copy claim something about the images that the images do not support?** Stock
   destination photography must never be described as client photographs, real trips, or
   anything "sent to us". Check what the asset actually is before writing a caption or a
   section intro around it. This has already shipped wrong once.
9. Does the call to action say enquire, plan, or tell us, and never book or buy?
10. Would Emma be comfortable reading this sentence out to a client on the phone? That is the
    real test, and it is the one she applied on the call.
